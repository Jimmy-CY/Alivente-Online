What is the Valuations page?

The Valuations page is the single source of truth for what each property was bought for and what it's worth today. One row per property, tracked on two axes:

  • Purchase Price — the amount originally paid. This is a historical anchor — it rarely changes after the initial capture.
  • Current Value — today's estimated market value. This should be kept up to date as valuations evolve (typically reviewed annually or when you get a fresh professional appraisal).
The 8 columns at a glance
  • Property — name of the property
  • m² — floor area (from the Properties module, not entered here)
  • Purchase Price — what you paid
  • Price/m² — calculated: Purchase Price ÷ m²
  • Current Value — today's market estimate
  • Value/m² — calculated: Current Value ÷ m²
  • Gain % — calculated: (Current − Purchase) ÷ Purchase × 100
  • Details — Edit button

A TOTAL row at the bottom aggregates the numerical columns across the whole portfolio, giving a single-glance view of total invested capital vs. current portfolio value.

Important — Current Value drives Pro-Rata expense distribution. When a property's Current Value changes, every Pro-Rata Expense that property is part of (e.g. Financials (Cyprus), Insurance (Cyprus), etc.) gets automatically recalculated. A property with a higher Current Value bears a larger share of each shared bill; a lower value means a smaller share. This is why keeping valuations up to date matters beyond mere reporting — it directly affects your P&L.

The system handles this cascade with a preview modal whenever a Current Value is edited — see the Add / Edit tab for the full walk-through.

A worked example

From your live portfolio — 13 properties, with a total purchase outlay of €4,041,000 and a current estimated value of €6,065,000. The portfolio has gained +50.1% since acquisition. Individual stories vary: Ionion - Villa H4 leads with +112.6% appreciation (€1,035k → €2,200k) while Spain - Eusebi Guell is still close to break-even at +10.5%. The Price/m² and Value/m² columns let you cross-compare properties of different sizes on a normalised basis.

Permissions: This page is restricted to Superusers.
Reading the Table

Each row represents one property. Some columns come straight from a stored value, others are computed on the fly from other fields. Here's the breakdown.

Stored fields
  • Property — the property name, from the Properties module.
  • m² — the floor area, sourced from the Property record's prop_floor_area field. If it's not set on the Property, you'll see a grey dash (—) here and the Price/m² and Value/m² columns for that row will also show dashes. Fix by going to Properties in the sidebar and editing the property.
  • Purchase Price — from this page's edit form.
  • Current Value — from this page's edit form.
Calculated fields
  • Price/m² = Purchase Price ÷ m² — what you paid per square metre. Useful for benchmarking historical acquisition value.
  • Value/m² = Current Value ÷ m² — today's market rate per square metre. Useful for cross-property benchmarking and sanity-checking whether a valuation feels right.
  • Gain % = (Current − Purchase) ÷ Purchase × 100 — percentage change in property value since acquisition. Shown as +X.X% for gains and −X.X% for losses.
Colour conventions
  • Green — positive gain (Current Value higher than Purchase Price)
  • Red — negative gain / loss (Current Value lower than Purchase Price)
  • Grey dash (—) — calculation not possible because a required input is missing (usually m² from the Property record, or one of the two values).
The TOTAL row

Aggregates across the portfolio:

  • Purchase Price TOTAL = sum of all Purchase Prices
  • Current Value TOTAL = sum of all Current Values
  • Gain % TOTAL = (Current TOTAL − Purchase TOTAL) ÷ Purchase TOTAL × 100 — your portfolio-level performance indicator

Note that the portfolio Gain % is not an average of the individual Gain percentages — it's weighted by value. A €2M property that gained 50% contributes more to the portfolio number than a €200k property that gained 100%.

Properties without a valuation don't appear. A property only shows up once you've captured at least Purchase Price and Current Value via Add New. If a property from your portfolio is missing here, it's never been valued.
Adding a Valuation

Click Add New in the top-right. The form has three fields:

  1. Property — dropdown of properties from the Properties module. Pick the one you're valuing. A property can only have one valuation record — if this property is already in the list, use Edit instead.
  2. Purchase Price — the amount originally paid, in euros.
  3. Current Value — today's estimated market value, in euros.

Click Save. The property now appears in the listing.

m² comes from the Property record. This form doesn't ask for floor area because it's already stored on the Property itself. The m² (and therefore Price/m² and Value/m²) will populate automatically in the listing if the Property has prop_floor_area set.
Editing a Valuation

Click the Edit button on any row. The form opens with three fields:

  • Property — locked (can't be changed on edit — a valuation belongs to one property for its entire life)
  • Purchase Price — editable but rarely changed (it's historical)
  • Current Value — the field you'll usually be updating
What happens when you change Purchase Price only

A simple save. Purchase Price is historical and doesn't drive any downstream calculations — it's purely for reporting. Changing it updates the Price/m² and Gain % on the listing; nothing else is affected.

What happens when you change Current Value

This is the important one. Current Value drives Pro-Rata expense distribution — so changing it has downstream effects that the system handles automatically.

When you change the Current Value and click Save, one of two things happens:

Case 1 — This property is in zero Pro-Rata distributions:

The valuation saves directly with no modal. Nothing else is affected. This is typical for properties whose expenses are all standard (electricity, property manager, communal fees) rather than shared-bill pro-rata ones.

Case 2 — This property is in one or more Pro-Rata distributions:

A preview modal opens showing every Pro-Rata distribution the property touches. Each distribution is a collapsible group (click the header to expand) listing every property in that distribution with:

  • CV (old) and CV (new) — the edited property's new Current Value is shown in teal; all other properties keep their CV unchanged
  • Share % — each property's new proportional slice of the distribution
  • Old Amount and New Amount — the per-month expense values before and after
  • Δ (delta) — the change per property, colour-coded: red if paying more, green if paying less

The edited property's row is highlighted yellow with an edited badge so it's always easy to find.

The buttons on the preview modal
  • Cancel — closes the modal without making any changes. You stay on the Edit form and can adjust or abandon.
  • Save & Recalculate All — commits the new Current Value and updates every affected Pro-Rata Expense record in one atomic operation. A success message confirms how many records were updated.
A note on historical P&L figures. Expense records aren't dated — they describe an ongoing pattern. Changing Current Value today will shift historical P&L numbers too. This is normal for budgeted expenses (which is what the Pro-Rata system captures). If you've exported a P&L report externally in the past, that export remains accurate as a point-in-time snapshot, but the live system will now reflect the updated distribution.
A small rounding note. The modal's column totals may occasionally show a cent or two drift (e.g. €3,000.01 instead of €3,000.00) because each property's new amount is rounded to two decimal places individually. This is cosmetic and reflects the actual amounts that get written to the database.
Practical Tips
Keeping valuations current
  • Review Current Values at least annually. A stale Current Value means a stale Pro-Rata distribution — and therefore a slightly wrong P&L. Set a reminder for a January review of every property valuation based on recent market data or professional appraisals.
  • Update Current Value after any major valuation event. New professional appraisal, significant renovation, comparable sale in the area, or market shift — all are valid reasons to update. The preview modal makes the downstream effect transparent so you can update with confidence.
  • Purchase Price is historical — treat it as read-only in practice. The Purchase Price is what you actually paid, including any transaction costs captured at the time. Don't revise it retroactively — it's meant to be an immutable reference point. If it's wrong from initial capture, fix it once; otherwise, leave it.
Using the calculated columns
  • Benchmark via Price/m² and Value/m². These columns normalise across property sizes. A €200,000 flat of 77m² is actually more expensive per square metre than a €450,000 villa of 186m² — something the absolute values hide but the /m² columns make instantly visible.
  • Watch for sudden Gain % spikes. A property that jumps from +25% to +80% between reviews deserves a sanity check — either your valuation source moved dramatically, or there's a data entry error. The sudden change is what to watch for.
  • Use the TOTAL row as a portfolio-level health indicator. The overall Gain % is the single number that tells you whether your property investment portfolio has appreciated. A +50% portfolio-level gain over N years can be compared against other investment classes as a rough return benchmark.
Missing data — fixing the dashes
  • A dash in m² means the Property record needs updating. Go to Properties in the sidebar, open the property, and set Floor Area. When you come back here, the dash becomes a number and both /m² columns populate automatically.
  • A dash in Price/m² or Value/m² always traces back to missing m² or missing values. Cross-check the row — if both Purchase Price and Current Value have numbers but the /m² columns show dashes, it's the m² that's missing, not the money.
  • A missing property from the listing altogether means no valuation has ever been captured. Click Add New and enter Purchase Price + Current Value to get it onto the listing.
The Pro-Rata connection
  • Properties without a Current Value can't be part of Pro-Rata distributions. Pro-Rata split is proportional to Current Value — a property without one has no basis for a share. Make sure every property intended for Pro-Rata has a valuation here first.
  • The cascade is transparent but not silent. Every time you edit a Current Value on a Pro-Rata property, the preview modal shows you exactly what's changing across which distributions, per property, with colour-coded deltas. Cancel freely during review.
  • For the reverse scenario — changing a bill amount — use the Line Type page instead. If the total amount of a shared bill has changed (say an audit fee went from €3,000 to €3,500), don't touch Current Values — edit the Pro-Rata Amount on the Line Type via Configuration → Expense Line Types. That triggers its own preview-and-cascade flow, designed for that specific case.
Valuations keep everything else honest. This page feels like a simple report, but it's the foundation of your Pro-Rata expense system. Accurate, current valuations mean accurate expense distribution, which means an accurate P&L. It's worth the annual maintenance effort.
What is the Profit & Loss Statement?

The P&L is the central financial report of Alivente Online — it pulls together every revenue stream and expense line across the portfolio to answer the core questions: How much money came in? How much went out? How much is left?

Everything you've set up in Configuration feeds into this one page:

  • Revenue Types + Line Types + Records → the Revenue section
  • Expense Types + Line Types + Records (including Pro-Rata distributions) → the Expenses section
  • Property Valuations → the Financial Indicators at the bottom (Gross ROI, Net ROI, % Value Increase)
  • Actual Expenses from the sidebar Expenses module → the Actual Expenses row (year mode only)
Always a year, then Budget or Actuals

The report is always for a specific year — the teal dropdown in the top-right picks which one, from your earliest lease year through to next year. Beside it, a two-button toggle picks what that year shows:

Budget (selected by default)

Shows only the budgeted numbers for the chosen year — the predictable, recurring pattern you've set up. This is the "what we expect to happen" view, and it is the only view that makes sense for a future year.

Actuals

Shows the budget plus actuals for that year. An extra Actual Expenses row appears above the Line Type rows, and Total Expenses / Net Profit include both. This is the "what actually happened" view — budget as the baseline, plus real-world unexpected expenses on top.

Past years are reported as they were

A past year shows the figures that were in force then, not today's. Change a budgeted amount with an Applies from date of today and last year's P&L does not move. A property you have since marked Inactive still reports in full for the years it was let. See the Expenses help for how the dating works.

The 14 columns

Twelve month columns (Jan-Dec), a Total column summing the year, and a Monthly column showing the year-average (Total ÷ 12). The Monthly column is particularly useful for comparing line items on a normalised basis — a €7,000 annual Company Tax and a €6,000 annual Property Manager bill both average to similar monthly figures despite being paid very differently.

Permissions: This page is restricted to Superusers.
Filtering the P&L

Two filter controls sit at the top of the page and let you slice the data in different ways.

The Year dropdown and the Budget / Actuals toggle

The year selector is the teal button in the top-right, showing the year you are looking at. It lists every year from your earliest lease through to next year, so you can look forward as well as back. Beside it sits the Budget / Actuals toggle.

These are two separate choices, and both survive a change of property selection — pick the year once, then narrow down by property without losing your place.

The Property Selection panel

Below the year dropdown, the Property Selection panel lists every property as a checkbox. All of them are selected when you first arrive. Tick or un-tick one and the whole report reloads on the new selection.

Three shortcuts:

  • Select All — ticks the active properties. This is your working portfolio.
  • + Inactive — ticks everything, inactive properties included. It only appears when you actually have an inactive property. Their earlier years still report in full, which is why they are worth including when you look back.
  • Select None — un-ticks everything (the report shows zeros until you re-select at least one).

Inactive properties carry an Inactive pill in the list so you can tell them apart at a glance. The counter shows e.g. "10 of 10 properties selected", so the scope you are looking at is never a guess.

The panel stays open while you pick. Ticking a property reloads the report, and the panel used to close every time. It now stays open — and keeps your scroll position — until you close it yourself or reload the page.
How the indicators recalculate

Change the selection and all five Financial Indicators recalculate on the new scope. Useful for comparing one country's portfolio against another, or isolating a single property's ROI.

One thing to know: the indicators do not simply divide by everything you ticked. A property that earned nothing and cost nothing in the selected year is left out of that year's denominators, and a line under the tiles says so by name. The Indicators tab explains why.

A practical workflow: start on Budget with everything selected to see the plan, then tick a single property for its individual Gross ROI and Net ROI, then just the Greek ones for the country-level picture. The report reshapes each time.
Reading the Grid

The grid is organised into four vertical sections, top to bottom:

1. REVENUE section

A teal header bar labels the section, then one row per Revenue Line Type that has activity in the selected period. You'll typically see:

  • Rental — core rental income, typically varies by season (summer months higher)
  • Levies — other monthly recurring income
  • Any other Line Types you've configured

The section closes with a bold TOTAL REVENUE row summing across all Line Types.

2. EXPENSES section

Another teal header bar, then:

In Year mode only: an Actual Expenses row appears at the top. This captures approved + paid one-off expenses from the sidebar Expenses module (unplanned repairs, ad-hoc bills, etc.) — see the Drilling In tab for the full explanation of how this is computed.

Then: one row per budgeted Expense Line Type with activity — sorted alphabetically (Agent Commission, Cleaning Costs, Communal Fees 1, Communal Fees 2, Company Tax, Electricity, … Water).

The section closes with a bold TOTAL EXPENSES row that is:

  • In Budget mode: sum of all budgeted Line Types
  • In Year mode: sum of budgeted Line Types + Actual Expenses
3. PROFIT section

Teal header bar, then a single bold NET PROFIT row calculated as:

Net Profit = Total Revenue − Total Expenses

So in Year mode, Net Profit naturally subtracts the Actual Expenses too, giving a true picture of what's left after both budgeted and real-world spending.

4. CONSOLIDATED FINANCIAL INDICATORS

Five KPI tiles below the grid — see the Indicators tab for the full explanation of each.

Total and Monthly columns
  • Total column — sums Jan through Dec for each row. This is the year figure.
  • Monthly column — Total ÷ 12. The average per-month figure. Useful for comparing lines with different payment patterns (e.g. a monthly €500 Property Manager fee vs a single €7,000 annual Company Tax — both "weigh" similarly per month, even though they hit very differently).
Rows you won't see

Line Types with no activity in the selected period are hidden automatically — this keeps the grid readable when you're viewing a filtered scope. If a Line Type you expected is missing, it means neither budgeted records nor actuals contributed any amount to this scope.

Drilling into any Number

Every blue number on the grid is clickable. Clicking opens a detail modal specific to the type of number you clicked.

Revenue Details modal

Click any blue number in the Revenue section. The modal shows:

  • The Line Type and month in the header
  • A table with two columns: Property and Amount (€)
  • One row per property that contributed to this Line Type in this month

Useful for answering "which property generated this revenue?" at a glance.

Budget Expense Details modal

Click any blue number in a budgeted Expense Line Type row. Structure mirrors the Revenue modal:

  • The Line Type and month in the header
  • A table: Property and Amount (€)
  • One row per property contributing to this Line Type in this month

For Pro-Rata Line Types (like Financials (Cyprus)), you'll see each property with its distributed share — matching the distribution you set up in Configuration.

Actual Expense Details modal

Click any blue number in the Actual Expenses row (year mode only). The modal shows a fuller table:

  • Date — the actual date the expense was incurred
  • Property — plus a green invoice icon if an invoice is attached
  • Description — free-text description of what the expense was for
  • Amount — the amount paid
Important — only Approved AND Paid actual expenses show here.

The Actual Expenses row and this detail modal include only actual expenses that have been both approved and marked paid. Draft expenses, pending-approval expenses, and approved-but-not-yet-paid expenses are not counted — they'll only appear once their full approval and payment workflow is complete.

If an expense you expect to see is missing from the P&L, the most likely explanation is that it's still somewhere in the approval/payment pipeline. Check its status on the sidebar Expenses module.

The Invoice Viewer

Clicking the green invoice icon next to a property name opens a nested modal (stacked on top of the Actual Expense Details) showing the attached invoice:

  • PDF files — embedded inline; fallback link provided if the browser can't render it
  • Image files (JPG/PNG) — displayed inline as an image
  • Other formats — can't be previewed; use the Download button

A Download button in the footer lets you save the file locally. Closing the Invoice Viewer returns you to the Actual Expense Details modal exactly where you were (scroll position preserved).

The Five Financial Indicators

Below the grid, five tiles summarise the financial health of the selected scope. They recalculate whenever you change the year or the property selection.

Which properties count — read this first

Each of these indicators divides a year's money by a fixed figure — what you paid for the property, how big it is, what it is worth. So the question "which properties belong in the divisor?" is a question about that year, not about today.

The rule: a property that earned nothing and cost nothing in the selected year is left out of that year's denominators.

That sounds severe until you notice it can only ever catch two kinds of property:

  • One you no longer hold — sold, or taken back for personal use, and marked Inactive. It was not part of the portfolio that year.
  • One you had not bought yet — look at 2022 for a flat you bought in 2024 and it has nothing against it, because it was not yours.

It cannot quietly hide an underperformer. A property you do hold always has expenses against it — management fees, taxes, insurance — even when it is standing empty. So it stays in, and the poor return it drags down is a real one you should be seeing.

Nothing is left out silently. When a property is excluded, a line appears under the tiles: "Based on 8 of 10 selected properties. Left out of 2027 because nothing was earned or spent on them: Dikaiosynis, Ionion Villa H4." If you see no such line, every property you ticked is in every figure.
1. Gross ROI

Formula: Total Revenue ÷ Purchase Price of the contributing properties × 100

What it tells you: how much revenue your investment generates relative to what you paid for it, before any expenses. A Gross ROI of 5% means you are generating 5% of your outlay in gross rental income per year. A top-line efficiency measure.

2. Net ROI

Formula: (Total Revenue − Total Expenses) ÷ Purchase Price of the contributing properties × 100

What it tells you: your real return — what percentage of the purchase price you actually keep after all costs. On the Actuals toggle, Total Expenses includes Actual Expenses, which makes this the most honest of the five.

Above 3–4% for a residential portfolio is typically healthy; above 6% is strong. Worth comparing against bonds or equities as a rough benchmark.

3. Expenses to Revenue

Formula: Total Expenses ÷ Total Revenue × 100

What it tells you: what share of every revenue euro is consumed by expenses. 30% means 30 cents of every euro goes out and 70 remain as gross operating margin. Lower is better, and a ratio creeping up year on year is the signal to watch — costs growing faster than revenue.

This one is deliberately not filtered. Both its halves are the same year's money, so a property that contributed nothing adds zero to each and cancels out on its own. Filtering it would change nothing except your confidence in it.
4. Rent (€/m2)

Formula: (Total Revenue ÷ 12) ÷ floor area of the contributing properties

What it tells you: average monthly revenue per square metre. Useful for benchmarking against local market rates and for comparing one country's portfolio against another on a normalised basis. A property standing empty all year still counts its square metres, because the space is real whether or not it is let.

5. % Value Increase

Formula: (value at the end of the selected year − purchase price) ÷ purchase price × 100

What it tells you: capital appreciation since purchase — the asset growth half of your return, alongside the income half that Gross and Net ROI measure. A portfolio with modest Net ROI but strong Value Increase is growing in capital terms even when yields look lean.

Two things make this one different from the others:

  • It reads the valuation in force at the end of the year you are viewing, not today's. Open 2022 and you see what the portfolio was worth at the end of 2022. This comes from the Effective From dates on Property Valuations, so keeping those accurate is what makes past years meaningful.
  • A property with no valuation dated that year or earlier drops out of both halves — its value and its purchase price. That has to happen together. Take out the value but leave the cost in, and a portfolio that has grown can be made to look as though it has shrunk, purely because one property has no valuation on file.
So this tile can cover fewer properties than the other four, and when it does the note underneath says so: "% Value Increase covers 6 of 8". It shows up most on older years, because valuation history has to start somewhere. Add an earlier-dated valuation on the Property Valuations page and the year fills in.
Reading them together: Net ROI + % Value Increase give you the total return picture — income plus capital growth. A property returning 4% Net ROI with 15% Value Increase has effectively returned 19% over the relevant period.
Practical Tips
Getting insight from the report
  • Pick the year, then flip Budget to Actuals. Budget is "what should happen", Actuals is "what did happen", and the difference between them is where the story is — unbudgeted expenses, gaps in revenue. On a future year only Budget means anything.
  • Compare the same indicator across years. Now that each year is reported on its own terms — its own figures, its own properties, its own valuations — stepping the year dropdown back through time is a genuine trend rather than today's numbers relabelled.
  • Use the Monthly column to compare rows on equal footing. A €7,000 Company Tax paid once a year and a €6,000 Property Manager fee paid monthly both have monthly averages of ~€500-€580 — comparable in scale even though they hit the grid very differently.
  • Watch for seasonal patterns in Revenue. Looking at the Rental row across the 12 months shows the seasonality of your portfolio — summer months typically higher, winter lower. A property with less seasonality than others might be worth investigating as a stability asset.
  • Filter to a single property for a focused story. Selecting one property in the Property Selection panel gives you that property's individual P&L, Net ROI, and value increase. Great for property-level analysis or answering "is this specific investment performing?"
  • Use country-level filtering to compare jurisdictions. Select just the Cyprus properties, then just the Greek properties, and compare Net ROI side-by-side. Differences can reveal tax burden, rental yield, or cost structure differences between markets.
Reconciling budget vs actuals
  • Actual Expenses only include approved AND paid items. If a number looks lower than expected, it's very likely some expenses are still in Draft or Pending Approval status on the Expenses module. Check them there.
  • The budgeted Line Type rows never include actuals. Each budgeted Line Type row is pure plan, unchanged across Budget and Year modes. Only the dedicated Actual Expenses row and the TOTAL EXPENSES row reflect the actuals addition.
  • If Net Profit looks very different from Budget Net Profit for the same year, it's the actuals doing it. Click into the Actual Expenses row for each month to see what's been paid — one big unplanned expense can shift the whole year's story.
Fixing numbers that look wrong
  • Revenue too low? Check Configuration → Revenues: is every property's Rental captured? Is the correct Revenue Type applied (so the amount is counted in the right months)?
  • Budgeted Expense too low? Configuration → Expenses: same check — property coverage, correct Expense Type for the timing, correct amount.
  • Pro-Rata distribution looks off? Drill into the Budget Expense Details modal for that Line Type — the per-property amounts should reflect Current Value proportions. If they don't, the Pro-Rata Amount or some Current Value is stale. Edit them via Configuration → Expense Line Types or via Property Valuations — both trigger the automatic cascade.
  • Financial Indicators showing "N/A"? Either Purchase Price or floor area is missing — check Property Valuations for Purchase Price and the Properties module for floor area (m²) — or no property in your selection contributed anything to the year, which leaves nothing to divide by.
  • An indicator changed and you did not expect it to? Read the line under the tiles. It names any property left out of the year, and reports separately how many properties % Value Increase covers. If there is no line, nothing was excluded and the answer is elsewhere.
  • % Value Increase says it covers fewer properties than the rest? Those properties have no valuation dated that year or earlier — usually on an older year, before your valuation history begins. Add a valuation with an earlier Effective From date on Property Valuations and the year fills in. This is not a fault; it is the report declining to guess.
This is your financial dashboard. The P&L is where everything else you've set up — Configuration, Valuations, even the sidebar Expenses — finally converges into a single story. Keep the upstream data tidy, and this page is reliable.
What is this report?

The Forecasted Cashflows dashboard shows your portfolio's expected money in against money out over the next 12 months, month by month. It pairs projected revenue (from your leases and seasonal income) against projected outflows (your budgeted expenses) to give you a rolling net position — and, crucially, to show you the months where money out is set to exceed money in before they arrive.

Revenue is on by default — but optional

At the top of the page is an “Include revenue (net cashflow)” switch. It is on by default, giving you the full net view. Turn it off and the page reverts to a pure outflows-only forecast (the classic Forecasted Cash Outflows behaviour), with the heading, legend and chart all switching to outflows only.

Two sides, two sources
  • Income (revenue) is derived from your leases (rent + levies for each month a signed lease covers) plus any seasonal / direct income (e.g. Ionion). It uses the same lease engine as the Rent-Roll and the P&L, so the figures reconcile.
  • Outflows come from your budgeted Expense records in Financials → Configuration → Expenses, projected forward month by month. These are the predictable, recurring, yearly-repeatable costs (Property Manager fees, Communal Fees, annual audit, Company Tax, etc.).
Two things to keep in mind.

1. Outflows are a budget forecast, not actual paid expenses. Approvals, invoice statuses and unexpected one-off costs are not here — for those use the Expenses module in the sidebar. 2. Income defaults to contracted leases only. When a lease expires with no successor captured, that income simply drops out of the forecast — that drop-off is the cash cliff. You can layer in assumed renewals with the slider (see the Revenue & the Slider tab).

Rolling 12-month horizon

The forecast always starts with today's current month and projects 12 months forward, rolling automatically each month. You never set dates yourself.

“Net” here means movement, not a bank balance. Net is simply income − outflows for each month. It does not track a running account balance or an opening figure — it shows whether each month is self-funding on its own.
Permissions: restricted to users with Financials access.
Reading the Dashboard
The three summary cards

The cards are cumulative planning horizons, each starting from now, and each shows Revenue, Expenses and Net (net turns red when negative). The exact month range each card covers is printed in its header.

  • Current Month — just this month (e.g. Aug 2026).
  • Next 3 Months — months 1–3 combined (e.g. Aug – Oct 2026).
  • Next 12 Months — the whole window (e.g. Aug 2026 – Jul 2027).
Because the horizons are cumulative they overlap — the Next-12 card is not the sum of the other two, it is the full 12 months (and it matches the net summary line below the cards).
The net summary line

Just under the cards a single line reads, for example, “Projected 12-month net: €16,873 · first cash-negative month: Jan 2027”. The first cash-negative month is the earliest month whose own income falls short of its outflows — your first pinch-point.

The 12-month chart

With revenue on, each month shows two bars side by side: a teal income bar (money in) and a red outflow bar (money out). Below each pair the label shows the month and that month's Net, coloured green for positive and red for negative — so a glance across the chart shows exactly which months dip into the red and where the income cliffs fall.

With revenue off, the chart collapses to a single set of outflow bars in one colour, the legend reads simply Outflows, and the chart title changes to Forecasted Outflows (12 Months).

Gridlines and scale

The Y-axis auto-scales to whichever series is taller (income or outflows) so nothing clips. Gridline labels (e.g. €10,000) appear down the left for reference.

Contracted vs assumed income

The income side is deliberately conservative by default: it counts only the rent from signed leases, plus seasonal / direct income. Income from a lease that expires with no successor captured is treated as at risk and is left out — which is what makes the cliffs visible.

The Renewal assumption slider

The slider runs from Contracted only (0%) to Assume renewals (100%). It lets you layer that at-risk income back in:

  • At 0% — the conservative floor. Only signed-lease and seasonal income. Expiring leases drop out.
  • At 100% — every expiring lease is assumed to renew at its current rent, projected forward.
  • In between — that percentage of the at-risk income is counted. Everything on the page (bars, cards, net, first-cash-negative month) updates live as you drag.
What a value literally means. For each month, income = contracted + seasonal + (slider % × the expiring-lease income). So 75% means “count three-quarters of the rent that's riding on expiring leases.” It is an aggregate haircut on that income, not a per-lease prediction — a sensitivity dial, not a forecast of which specific tenants renew.
How to use it
  • Stress test. Leave it at 0% to see the worst case, push to 100% for the best case — the gap between them is your renewal risk.
  • Find the break-even. Slide down until a month flips negative: that tells you the renewal rate below which that month runs short.
  • Model your reality. If you typically retain, say, ~85% of tenants, sit the slider around there for a realistic middle view.

The slider only appears when revenue is switched on.

  • Outflow bar — that month's expenses grouped by Expense Line Type, with a total.

  2. Click a Line Type row to drill into the property-level split — each property's amount and its percentage of that Line Type (this is your Pro-Rata distribution). A Back to Monthly View button returns you to the list.

Practical Tips
Planning with the net view
  • Start from the first cash-negative month. The summary line names the earliest month that dips into the red on the contracted floor. That's the one to plan a reserve for — or to chase renewals ahead of.
  • Watch the income cliffs. A month where the teal income bar drops well below its neighbours usually means a lease is expiring with no successor yet captured. Hover it to confirm which tenant, then get the renewal moving.
  • Use the slider as a risk gauge. The distance between the 0% view and the 100% view is your renewal exposure. If the picture only looks healthy near 100%, you are leaning heavily on renewals that aren't signed yet.
If the income looks wrong
  • A month reads lower than expected — check that the lease covering it is captured in Tenants with the right start/end dates and rent + levies. Uncovered months show no lease income by design.
  • Seasonal income missing — confirm the property's income rows are set on the Revenue screens for the relevant months.
If the outflows look wrong
  • Too low — an Expense record is probably missing in Configuration → Expenses.
  • Too high — look for a wrong Expense Type (e.g. a quarterly cost set to Monthly) or a double-count; drill into the Line Type to find it.
  • A Pro-Rata total is off — check the Line Type's Pro-Rata Amount, or a property's Current Value (a stale value skews the split).
General awareness
  • It reflects your data instantly. Changes to leases, revenue rows or Expense records show on the next page load — no refresh or rebuild.
  • One-off costs won't appear. Ad-hoc repairs and supplier invoices live in the Expenses module, not this budget forecast.
  • Income reconciles with the P&L and Rent-Roll. The same lease engine feeds all three, so a contracted figure here should match what you see there.
This is your 12-month cash radar. Keep leases and Configuration tidy and it becomes a reliable early warning — both for big bills and for income cliffs — so neither arrives as a surprise.

The Financial Indicators dashboard is the benchmarking counterpart to the Profit & Loss Statement. Where the P&L tells you "how is the portfolio doing?", this report tells you "how is each property doing compared to the rest of the portfolio?"

The scoreboard columns

The Detailed Property Data table has a Rank and Score up front, then seven metric columns. All figures are for the selected year.

Rank & Score

The overall standing of each property within your current selection. How they're worked out is explained in the Scoring & Rank tab. In short: Score is a 0–100 blend of the property's rank on the scoring metrics; Rank orders those scores (best = #1).

Gross ROI (%)

Revenue ÷ Purchase Price × 100. Top-line yield before costs. Higher is better.

Net ROI (%)

(Revenue − Expenses) ÷ Purchase Price × 100. The real return after expenses. Higher is better. Shown and colour-graded, but NOT part of the Score (Gross ROI + Expenses-to-Revenue already capture it — see Scoring & Rank).

Expenses to Revenue (%)

Expenses ÷ Revenue × 100. What share of income goes to costs. Lower is better.

A held property that earned €0 revenue that year shows ∞ here (you can't divide by zero income) and is graded worst on this column — so an empty year is never rewarded for having "no cost ratio".
Rent (€/m2)

(Revenue ÷ 12) ÷ floor area. Monthly rent per square metre — the size normaliser. Higher is better. Shown and graded, but NOT part of the Score (it's a characteristic of the property, not a performance result).

Value Increase (%)

(Value at year-end − Purchase Price) ÷ Purchase Price × 100. Capital appreciation. Higher is better.

Now effective-dated. Value Increase uses the property value in force at the end of the selected year, not just today's value. Record dated valuations on the Property Valuations page (each save has an Effective From date) and each year picks up the right one. A year before a property's first recorded valuation shows N/A and is left out of that year's Score.
Occupancy (%)

Share of the year the property was let, counting a month if a signed lease or an assumed continuation covers it — the same basis revenue uses, so a steady renewer isn't penalised for a not-yet-captured renewal. Seasonal / no-lease properties show "Seasonal" (occupancy isn't meaningful for them) and are left out of that property's Score. Higher is better.

Rent Growth (%)

Change in average monthly rent vs last year — revenue ÷ months actually held, this year against last year. Using the monthly rate (not total revenue) means a first full year after a part-year start reads as the true change, not a phantom jump. A property with no full prior year shows N/A and is left out of that year's Score. Higher is better.

"not held this year"

A property that wasn't held in the selected year (before its first lease) is shown greyed with a "not held this year" tag and a dash in every column. It's excluded from the ranking and from the Portfolio Totals, so historical years aren't distorted by properties you didn't own yet.

How the Score and Rank are worked out
Rank-based, within your selection

For each scoring metric, the held properties in your current selection are placed best→worst and given a position grade from 0 (best) to 1 (worst). This "rank-based" approach is robust to outliers — one enormous number can't dominate the way it would with raw values. Everything is relative to the currently-selected, held properties, so the numbers move as you change the selection or the year.

The five scoring metrics

The Score blends five metrics, equal weight by default:

In the Score: Gross ROI · Expenses to Revenue · Occupancy · Rent Growth · Value Increase
Shown & graded, but NOT scored: Net ROI and Rent (€/m²). Net ROI is excluded to avoid double-counting (Gross ROI and Expenses-to-Revenue already capture it); Rent/m² is a property characteristic, not a performance result.

Score = the (weighted) average of (1 − grade) across those five metrics, × 100. A metric that doesn't apply to a property (Seasonal occupancy, N/A rent growth, no dated valuation) is simply skipped for that property — not counted as zero. Rank then orders the scores, best first; properties with the same score share a rank (1, 2, 2, 4).

Ties don't swing the ranking

When several properties share the same value on a metric (e.g. everyone at 100% occupancy, or two both at 0% rent growth), they all get the same grade rather than being split into a winner and a loser by row order. A metric on which everyone is equal simply washes out and doesn't affect the ranking.

Scoring weights

Open "Scoring weights" at the top of the Detailed Property Data section to change how much each metric counts. Set a weight from 0 to 5 (0 ignores the metric entirely, 1 is normal) and the Score and Rank recompute live. A custom badge appears when weights aren't equal; Reset to equal puts them back. Your choice is remembered on this device.

If one metric feels like it's dominating — say a single negative Rent Growth is dragging an otherwise strong property down — dial that metric's weight down and watch the ranking settle. Weighting is the honest way to encode your view of what matters.

What is this report?

The Vacancy Management dashboard tracks how well your properties stay rented over time. Where the Financial Indicators dashboard shows you income performance, this one shows you the occupancy side of the story — which complements rather than duplicates income analysis.

Three occupancy KPIs are calculated for each property and benchmarked against the portfolio average:

  • Avg Days to Fill — how long it takes to re-let a property between tenants
  • Vacancy Cost — the revenue lost during empty periods
Opt-in — only "rental" properties appear
Not every property in your portfolio is shown on this page.

  • Owner-occupied properties — where you or your family live; no rental = no vacancy metric
  • Short-term / summer-let properties — where the let pattern is seasonal and comparing "% of the year occupied" against traditional long-term rentals would be misleading
  • Properties not yet rented or no longer rented — properties between investment phases

If you have 10 properties in the portfolio but only 9 appear on this page, it means 1 is flagged as excluded from occupancy tracking. Toggle the flag on the Properties module if you want it included.

The Three Occupancy Indicators

Each indicator captures a different dimension of occupancy performance. Read all three together for a complete picture.

1. Occupancy Rate (%)

Formula: (Days Occupied ÷ Total Days in Period) × 100

What it tells you: What percentage of the selected time period a property had a paying tenant. A property occupied for 330 out of 365 days in the past year has a 90.4% Occupancy Rate.

Higher is better. The industry benchmark for healthy long-term residential rentals is usually 95% or above.

2. Avg Days to Fill

Formula: Average number of days between one tenant moving out and the next tenant moving in, across all turnover events in the period.

What it tells you: How quickly a property re-lets when it becomes vacant. A property with an Avg Days to Fill of 15 days is turning around quickly; one with 60+ days is taking a long time (and costing you revenue).

Lower is better.

Why some properties show "0 days". A zero value doesn't mean a property filled instantly — it means there were no vacancy gaps to measure in the selected period. This can happen for three reasons:
  • The tenant renewed their lease — no gap, no measurement needed
  • Back-to-back tenants — the new tenant moved in the same day the previous one moved out (or the next day)
  • No turnover at all in the period — the same tenant has been there the whole time

In all three cases, 0 days is a good thing — shown as Excellent in the Performance Modal.

3. Vacancy Cost (€)

Formula: For each vacant day: Days Vacant × (Monthly Rent ÷ 30)

What it tells you: The actual revenue you didn't earn because of vacant periods. If a property rents for €900/month and sits vacant for 30 days, the Vacancy Cost is ~€900. If the same property sat vacant for 120 days, it would be ~€3,600 in lost revenue.

Which rent is used for the calculation? The system is intelligent about this:

  • If a new tenant has already been signed (but hasn't moved in yet), the next lease's rent is used — that's the rent you'll be collecting soon
  • If the property is still actively vacant, the previous lease's rent is used — that's the best estimate of what it would be earning if still let

Lower is better. This is the single most meaningful indicator because it translates vacancy into real money lost.

Performance tier thresholds
Excellent

Occupancy Rate: value is 100% — "Perfect" (the property has been occupied for the entire period).
Avg Days to Fill / Vacancy Cost: value is zero (Perfect) or 80% or less of the portfolio average.

Good

Occupancy Rate: 95% to 99.9% — strong but not perfect.
Avg Days to Fill / Vacancy Cost: 80% to 100% of the portfolio average.

Needs Improvement

Occupancy Rate: below 95%.
Avg Days to Fill / Vacancy Cost: more than 100% of the portfolio average.

Occupancy Rate uses absolute thresholds, not relative. Unlike the Financial Indicators page, where tiers are 80%/120% of portfolio average, Occupancy Rate has fixed absolute cutoffs: 100% = Excellent, 95-99.9% = Good, <95% = Needs Improvement. The reason: 95% is a well-established industry benchmark for healthy residential rentals, so measuring against the portfolio's own average could mask a problem (if the whole portfolio is under-occupied, you'd want every property flagged — not just the worst ones).

Practical Tips
Using the time period correctly
  • Start with Past Year for routine monitoring. It's the most immediately relevant window — what's been happening lately? A problem property in the past year is something you can act on now.
  • Use Last 3 Years to spot chronic issues. A property that's been in the Needs Improvement tier for three consecutive years has a structural problem, not a recent stroke of bad luck. Time to review the pricing, condition, location, or letting agent.
  • Use All Time for investment analysis. When considering whether to sell or hold a property, lifetime occupancy patterns are a better signal than the last 12 months. A property that's been 95%+ occupied for 5+ years is a strong asset; one that's been in the low 80s is worth examining.
Reading the three KPIs together
  • Vacancy Cost is the one that matters most. It translates occupancy directly into euros. A property with 85% Occupancy Rate might sound fine, but if the Vacancy Cost is €2,400, that's real money you didn't earn. Focus your attention wherever Vacancy Cost is highest.
  • Cross-reference Occupancy Rate with Avg Days to Fill. A property can have low occupancy for two very different reasons: (a) long individual vacancy gaps (slow to re-let), or (b) many short vacancy gaps (high tenant turnover). These need different solutions — (a) is a marketing / pricing problem, (b) is often a property-quality or management problem.
  • A high Vacancy Cost with low Avg Days to Fill is unusual. It would mean the property re-lets quickly but still has lots of vacancy — which usually indicates high turnover. Investigate why tenants aren't staying.
  • Zero values ("0 days", €0 cost) are the goal. They mean either back-to-back tenancy or a renewed lease — no gap at all. Celebrate the properties that consistently show zeros.
Managing the opt-in flag
  • Flag a property for occupancy tracking only if "% of the year occupied" is a meaningful measure for it. Long-term residential rentals: yes. Short-term holiday lets, owner-occupied homes, vacant land: no.
  • If a property's use changes, update the flag. A property that was a summer let last year and is now a long-term rental should be flagged on to start appearing on this page. Conversely, a rental that becomes a holiday let should be flagged off.
  • Where to toggle it: on the Properties module, edit the property and look for the "Include in Occupancy" checkbox.
Diagnosing Needs-Improvement properties
  • Vacancy Cost high and Avg Days to Fill high → slow-to-let problem. Likely pricing, marketing, or agent issues. Consider: is the rent above comparable market rates? Are the listing photos good? Is your letting agent proactive?
  • Occupancy Rate low but Avg Days to Fill low → high-churn problem. Tenants are leaving quickly. Why? Property condition? Rent inflation? Neighbourhood issues? Lease terms that don't attract stable tenants?
  • Occupancy Rate low and Vacancy Cost modest → low-rent property with chronic gaps. The revenue impact is limited by the low rent, but the property still isn't working. Consider whether it makes sense to keep it in the portfolio.
Acting on what you see
  • The View Historical Performance Trends button at the bottom of the page jumps to multi-year trend charts. Use it when a property looks problematic on this page and you want to see whether it's a recent regression or a long-term pattern.
  • Cross-reference with the Financial Indicators page. A property can have perfect occupancy but still have a poor Net ROI if expenses are eating into income. Use both dashboards together for the full picture.
  • Export Excel for stakeholder reviews. The Excel export from the Performance Modal is useful for periodic property portfolio reviews with accountants, investment partners, or co-owners.
This dashboard is about lost opportunity. Every day a property is vacant is a day you're not earning. The goal isn't just to spot underperformers — it's to translate the numbers into specific actions: adjust pricing, switch letting agent, address property condition, renew earlier, or consider repositioning the asset.

Reading the Three Line Charts

The top of the page shows three line charts, one per KPI, each covering the full year range. They all share the same layout:

  • X-axis: years (oldest on the left, current year on the right, labelled YTD)
  • Y-axis: the KPI value — percent, days, or euros
  • Main line with markers: the KPI value for each year
  • Red dashed line: the average across all the years shown — the benchmark for comparing individual years
Chart 1 — Occupancy Rate

Teal line with a filled area below. Higher is better. Years where the line is above the red dashed average are better-than-average years. Years below are worse-than-average years.

Chart 2 — Avg Days to Fill

Orange line with a filled area. Lower is better. Interpretation is reversed: years below the red dashed average are your good years (fast to re-let); years above are problem years (slow to re-let).

Chart 3 — Vacancy Cost

Pink line with a filled area. Lower is better. Same reversed interpretation as Days to Fill: below the average = good, above = bad. Spikes are years when vacancy cost you a lot of revenue.

Interacting with the charts
  • Hover a point — a tooltip shows the KPI value and a context figure (property count for Occupancy, vacancy count for the other two). Also prompts "Click for detailed breakdown".
  • Click a point — opens the Year Detail modal for that year, same as clicking the year in the table below
  • Hover the red line — shows the all-years average value
How to read trends
Upward trend on Occupancy Rate, downward trend on Days to Fill & Cost

The portfolio is improving. Whatever you've been doing (pricing, agent selection, property condition) is working. Keep going.

Downward trend on Occupancy, upward trend on Days to Fill & Cost

The portfolio is deteriorating. Investigate. Is it one problem property dragging the average, or a systemic issue? Click into recent years to diagnose.

Flat trend near or above/below the average

Stable performance. This is actually a good outcome — consistent, predictable occupancy.

Single-year spike or dip

One anomalous year surrounded by normal years. Click into that year to understand what happened — was it a specific property or event, or a broader problem that self-corrected?

The Yearly Data Summary table

Below the charts, the same data appears in a row-by-row format with additional raw data not shown on the charts. Every row is clickable and opens that year's detail modal.

The 8 columns
  • Year — the calendar year. Current year carries a blue YTD badge.
  • Occupancy % — same value shown in the Occupancy Rate chart, to one decimal place
  • Avg Days to Fill — same value shown in the Days to Fill chart
  • Vacancy Cost — same value shown in the Vacancy Cost chart, in euros with thousands separators
  • Properties — how many properties were tracked that year. This changes over time as properties are added to or removed from occupancy tracking
  • Vacancies — the count of vacancy events (not vacant days) that started or ended in the year
  • Available Days — the total days the tracked properties could have been rented in the year (roughly: property count × days in year, adjusted for partial-year properties)
  • Occupied Days — the total days the tracked properties were actually rented
The raw data lets you verify the KPIs

The Occupancy % column is literally Occupied Days ÷ Available Days × 100. So if you see 99.0% with 1,375 available days and 1,361 occupied days, you can verify: 1,361 ÷ 1,375 = 98.98%, which rounds to 99.0%. The raw numbers are shown for transparency.

Click any row for detail

Clicking anywhere on a year's row opens the Year Detail modal — the same behaviour as clicking a point on a chart. Hovering a row shows a subtle highlight so you know it's clickable.

The table and the charts show the same KPI values in different formats. The charts are best for spotting trends at a glance; the table is best for looking up exact numbers or investigating the underlying data (property count, vacancy count, raw days).
The Year Detail Modal

Click any year (in the table or on any chart) and a large modal opens with the full breakdown for that year. This is where you investigate why a year looked the way it did.

Period summary

Blue info bar at the top showing: the year (with "Year-to-Date" suffix for the current year), number of properties operational that year, and the number of vacancy periods.

Three metric cards

The same three KPIs shown in the trend charts, but now with additional context below each big number:

  • Occupancy Rate — shows "X / Y days" beneath the percentage, making the underlying math visible (e.g. "184 / 184 days" means 184 occupied out of 184 available)
  • Avg Days to Fill — shows the vacancy count, so "1.8 days over 3 vacancies" tells a different story than "1.8 days over 20 vacancies"
  • Vacancy Cost — labelled "Lost revenue" to reinforce what the number really represents
Property Performance Breakdown table

One row per property tracked that year, showing:

  • Property name
  • Available Days — days this property could have been rented in the year
  • Occupied Days — days it was actually rented
  • Vacant Days — the difference (Available − Occupied)
  • Occupancy Rate — shown as a colour-coded badge
The badge thresholds on this modal differ slightly from the Vacancy Management Performance Modal.

On this page:

  • Green — Occupancy ≥ 95%
  • Yellow — Occupancy between 85% and 94%
  • Red — Occupancy below 85%

These are wider tolerance bands for reviewing historical full-year data, where seasonal fluctuations naturally produce a broader spread.

Vacancy Periods table

Below the property breakdown, the individual vacancy events that occurred during the year:

  • Property — which property the vacancy happened on
  • Start Date — when the property became vacant
  • End Date — when a new tenant moved in (or the end of the period if still vacant)
  • Days — the length of the vacancy
  • Previous Tenant — the tenant who moved out
  • Next Tenant — the tenant who moved in (if filled)
  • Cost — the revenue lost during this specific gap, calculated from the rent of the next lease (if signed) or the previous lease (if still vacant)

If the year had no vacancies at all, this section shows a green banner: "No vacancies recorded for this period!" — a perfect year.

The Vacancy Periods table is the most actionable information on this page. It lets you pinpoint exactly when a property was vacant, for how long, between which tenants, and at what cost. When investigating a bad year, this is where you go.
Practical Tips
Reading trends properly
  • Trends matter more than single years. A single bad year might just be variance; a three-year deterioration is a pattern worth investigating. Don't panic after one bad year; don't celebrate after one good one.
  • YTD fairness — don't compare apples to oranges. The current year's number is a partial-year figure. Comparing "2026 YTD Vacancy Cost = €0" against "2025 full-year Vacancy Cost = €11,162" isn't meaningful in April 2026 — you haven't had time to accumulate vacancies yet.
  • The red dashed line is your benchmark. Years above the average on Occupancy are wins; years above the average on Days-to-Fill or Cost are problems. Memorise this — the interpretation reverses depending on the metric.
  • Look for regime changes. Did something change in a specific year? A new letting agent, a major renovation, a market downturn? The charts help you spot when things turned, and the Year Detail modal helps you diagnose why.
Investigating a specific year
  • Start with the metric cards. Before anything else, check the three metric cards to get the headline numbers. If "1.8 days over 3 vacancies" vs "1.8 days over 20 vacancies" — the second year is clearly worse even though the average is the same.
  • Property Performance Breakdown shows you the culprits. If one year had 90% portfolio occupancy but one property dragged it down to 61%, you'll see it immediately on the breakdown. That's where to focus.
  • Vacancy Periods reveals the individual events. A long single gap (e.g. 180 days) is a very different problem from ten short gaps (e.g. 18 days each). Long gaps = slow re-letting; short gaps = high churn. Different solutions.
  • Previous/Next Tenant columns can reveal patterns. Is the same tenant leaving repeatedly (implies short leases, maybe corporate tenants)? Is there always a gap before the same returning tenant (implies seasonal arrangement)? The names tell a story the numbers don't.
Using the page for decisions
  • Investor reports. A screenshot of the three trend charts is often the clearest way to communicate portfolio health to co-owners, family members, or financial advisors.
  • Property sale/hold decisions. A property that appears in multiple Year Detail modals as the main culprit — across different years — is a chronic problem, not a recent issue. That's the one you might consider selling.
  • Agent evaluation. If Days to Fill is trending up while nothing else has changed, the letting agent might be slowing down. If Days to Fill dropped dramatically in a specific year, that's worth understanding — did you change agent, or change something about pricing/presentation?
  • Plan improvements. The years with the highest Vacancy Cost on this page are the ones you don't want to repeat. What was different in your best years? Can you replicate those conditions?
Connecting back
  • The Back button returns to Vacancy Management. Use it when you've understood the trends and want to drill into a specific property's current-state metrics.
  • Vacancy Management for current state, Performance Trends for history. The two pages complement each other — Vacancy Management answers "how are we doing now?"; Performance Trends answers "how have we been doing over time?" Use both for a complete view.
  • The property count can reveal portfolio growth. If 2023 shows 1 property and 2026 shows 9 properties, that's the rate at which you've been adding properties to occupancy tracking. Growth itself can distort averages — a new addition with an opening-year vacancy can drag the whole portfolio's numbers down.
